0.087 Sample-simulate-select (S3) draws N candidates per prompt from a frozen text-to-motion...
Contributions
The paper introduces S3, a training-free pipeline that composes a frozen text-to-motion generator, a deterministic IK retargeter, and a physics simulator running the exact deployment tracking policy as a best-of-N selector. Because the simulator is the verifier, S3 attains the any-of-N ceiling by construction, letting the authors measure how much of the human-to-humanoid gap is closeable by selection alone versus requiring training. They show selection raises upright execution from 83.5% to 89.5% (and 80.5% to 89.5% on the full 4,184-prompt test split), that a cheap kinematic fall-predictor recovers only a quarter of this gain, and that all 177 gate-selected clips executed successfully on a real robot.
Applicable to our work
The core best-of-N-screened-by-a-frozen-deterministic-verifier pattern is exactly hexgen's own architecture, so this paper's evaluation protocol is directly transferable: measure the 'any-of-N ceiling' of the frozen hexgen decoder separately from what additional training buys, to see how much headroom sampling alone can close versus needing better training data. Its central finding -- that a cheap proxy/kinematic verifier with strong population-level classification accuracy (AUROC 0.90) still recovers only a quarter of the achievable gain because within-prompt ranking is harder than population classification -- is a direct caution against substituting a cheaper learned critic for hexgen's frozen machine-checkable verifier when doing best-of-N selection. The paper's method of isolating a specific unrecoverable failure class (squat/bend motions) and showing that only targeted training data (not more sampling) fixes it is a useful diagnostic pattern for identifying which classes of B-rep parts hexgen's decoder structurally cannot produce valid programs for, versus which just need more samples.
Additional context
Domain (humanoid motion control) is unrelated to CAD/mesh generation, so nothing here is directly reusable as code, but the evaluation methodology (ceiling measurement, kinematic-vs-execution verifier comparison, isolating unrecoverable failure classes) is a strong conceptual match to hexgen's own best-of-N + frozen verifier design and worth reading in full for the S3 experimental protocol (Section III-E, IV-B, IV-C, IV-D).

0.042 ProCredit reruns the task's existing acceptance checks after every turn during training...
Contributions
The paper proposes ProCredit, which converts a long-horizon agentic task's existing binary acceptance checks into a verified, environment-grounded progress signal read after every turn, rather than relying solely on the terminal outcome reward or a learned reward/critic model. This progress signal supplies both a trajectory-level advantage (separating failed attempts by how much they completed) and a turn-level advantage (crediting the specific turn where progress occurred), which the authors show via ablation is what drives the improvement, not merely having any progress signal at all.
Applicable to our work
hexgen already has a frozen, machine-checkable verifier (compiles, zero inverted elements, quality bounds, surface-match, feature-edge capture) -- ProCredit's core idea transfers directly: rerun a subset of that verifier's checks after each emitted block/sweep/projection command during autoregressive generation to get a fraction-of-checks-passed progress signal, then use the *change* in that fraction between consecutive generation steps as a dense, per-command RL reward. This would let an RL fine-tuning stage over hexgen's decoder credit the specific command that first produces an inverted element or breaks surface-match fidelity, rather than only rewarding/penalizing the whole best-of-N candidate at the end -- directly addressing the 'self-play/verifier-in-the-loop training' care area, and it would make all-fail best-of-N batches (no candidate compiles) still informative during RL fine-tuning, which outcome-only training currently discards.
Additional context
Domain is text/tool-use agentic RL (AppWorld app-automation tasks), not geometric/program-mesh generation, so the transfer is at the mechanism level only. Key caveat from the paper's own discussion: progress must come from the environment state, not from the policy's own predictions, or group normalization lets the policy game the signal without improving task success -- for hexgen this means incremental verifier checks used for reward must run against actual partial program execution/mesh state, not a learned proxy. The method also assumes checks are meaningful on partial/intermediate states; hexgen's verifier currently seems designed to check only completed candidates, so some checks (e.g. inverted-elements-so-far, partial surface coverage) would need engineering to be well-defined mid-generation.

Applicable to our work

0.014 CANOPY frames each tree-structured decision problem (model routing, prefix caching, tes...
Contributions
The paper introduces CANOPY, a multi-fidelity tree bandit that certifies local smoothness directly from cheap random-path probes rather than assuming or searching over a global smoothness schedule, and directs expensive leaf evaluations toward cells where the certificate detects a violation. It proves fixed-budget identification and regret guarantees whose extra cost over the smooth-tree rate is additive in the number of discontinuities, and shows empirically across twelve benchmarks (routing, top-k identification, test-time search, caching, prompt trimming) that this consistently improves matched-budget performance, including 2.9x higher top-10 recall on a 1000-model pool and 1.6x more SWE-bench Verified issues resolved than best-of-N.
Applicable to our work
CANOPY's core mechanism — treat autoregressive generation as a tree where every prefix is a node, use cheap biased internal-node signals to certify where a smoothness/value assumption locally holds, and concentrate expensive exact evaluation (here, the frozen structural-validity verifier) only on flagged, uncertain prefixes — is a directly transferable template for MCTS/beam-style value-guided search over hexgen's program prefixes: rather than a fixed rollout/verification budget per node, a data-driven certificate could decide where cheap heuristic scores (e.g. partial-program feasibility estimates) are trustworthy versus where a full verifier run (compile + mesh + quality check) is needed, reducing best-of-N verifier calls at matched compute.
Additional context
Applied entirely to LLM-serving and search settings (routing, caching, trimming, math/code search), not to program or geometry generation, so the bridge is purely at the level of the tree-search/certification algorithm, not any domain-specific mechanism. The guarantees assume leaf-mean sub-Gaussianity and a dispersion condition on discontinuities (Balcan et al., 2018) that would need to be checked or approximated for the very different reward landscape of mesh-program validity.

0.010 Two randomly-initialized transformers co-evolve: a generator proposes byte-emitting pro...
Contributions
The paper introduces Self-Play Pretraining with Zero Data, in which a generator and learner bootstrap entirely from random initialization with no natural data, using a universal Turing machine as the program space and a learning-progress RL reward to keep the generator's outputs at the frontier of the learner's capability. Across held-out natural datasets spanning text, images, audio, speech, music, and DNA, the resulting learner exhibits predictable zero-shot power-law loss scaling in self-play compute, with exponents comparable to direct training on natural data, plus emergent in-context learning and discovery of recognizable mathematical program families (arithmetic, Fibonacci, geometric, quadratic, cubic sequences) during training.
Applicable to our work
The core mechanism — a generator trained by RL with a learning-progress reward to produce synthetic (input, target) pairs at the frontier of what the learner can currently master, replacing a fixed/random data-generating prior with an adaptive curriculum — is directly transferable to hexgen's 'inverse factory': instead of sampling uniformly random valid programs, an RL-trained generator could be shaped to propose B-rep/program pairs whose difficulty tracks the decoder's current capability, potentially improving data efficiency and coverage versus the current random-sampling data synthesis strategy. The self-play program pool's mix of fresh samples, mutations of high-reward programs, and experience replay is also a reusable recipe for curriculum-managing synthetic (B-rep, program) generation.
Additional context
This is a pretraining-methodology paper (predicting raw bytes from a universal Turing machine), not about B-rep encoders, pointer/copy mechanisms, or mesh validity; the transferable idea is specifically the adaptive, gradient-alignment-based learning-progress curriculum for synthetic data generation, not the architecture or byte-level tokenization itself. The learning-progress reward computation (Eq. 2) requires access to per-program learner gradients, which may be a nontrivial engineering cost to replicate for a 27M-parameter program-generation setting.

Contributions
The paper presents Discovery Loop, a minimal, open-source, single-machine reimplementation of the AlphaEvolve-style LLM-guided program evolution loop that uses one LLM call per iteration with no distributed infrastructure. Applied to the Packomania csqv circle-packing benchmark, it produced new best-known solutions for 10 of 12 targets (N=101-114), improving on prior records by 2.4%-5.4%, independently verified and accepted by Packomania, at a total LLM cost of $27.72 over 15 iterations in about 8 hours on a consumer PC. The authors also analyze cost-efficiency dynamics across iterations and propose a plateau-detection mechanism that would have cut cost by 50% with negligible quality loss, and catalog several non-trivial algorithmic ideas (e.g. hexagonal lattice initialization, defect migration) the LLM discovered on its own.
Applicable to our work
The core loop — LLM proposes a full candidate program, an independent zero-tolerance (no-shared-code) verifier checks it, only verified improvements are kept, with a scoreboard/history fed back into the next prompt — is structurally analogous to our best-of-N + frozen-verifier screening setup (problem 6, self-play/verifier-in-the-loop training) and to problem 1 (structural validity via execution/verifier-guided generation), though here the 'decoder' is an LLM prompted in natural language rather than our trained graph-transformer. The 'full replacement over patch' design choice and the per-target-best-tracking-separate-from-champion idea could inform how we structure rejection-sampled SFT rounds or best-of-N bookkeeping across many parts. The plateau-detection mechanism (windowed diminishing-returns stopping) is a directly transferable, cheap idea for deciding when to stop a best-of-N sampling budget or a self-play round for a given part/target.
Additional context
This is an LLM-agent (prompted-program-search) paper, not a trained neural decoder, so the applicability is at the level of loop design and verifier discipline rather than architecture. Results (circle packing record-breaking) are in a very different domain (continuous geometric optimization via classical numerical methods) and the 'verifier' here is a simple feasibility/overlap checker, much simpler than our scaled-Jacobian / surface-fidelity verifier — the core principle (independent, zero-tolerance, no-shared-code checker) is the transferable piece.
